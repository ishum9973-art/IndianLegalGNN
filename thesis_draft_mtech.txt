TITLE: FusionCaseGNN for Graph-Based Legal Case Retrieval on the FIRE 2017 IRLeD Dataset: An M.Tech Thesis Draft Based on the CaseGNN Codebase

ABSTRACT

Legal case retrieval is an important problem in judicial information systems because relevant precedents must be identified from a large collection of prior decisions. This thesis studies a graph-based legal retrieval pipeline implemented in the present codebase and adapts its description to the FIRE 2017 IRLeD precedent retrieval task. The main research question is whether a text-attributed graph representation of legal cases can provide a stronger retrieval framework than purely lexical or single-encoder baselines for precedent search. Beyond reconstructing the original pipeline, the thesis introduces a novelty-enhanced model named FusionCaseGNN. FusionCaseGNN extends the base graph retrieval design with two lightweight but effective mechanisms: learnable fusion of fact-view and issue-view graph similarities, and weighted fusion between graph retrieval scores and BM25 lexical relevance. The implemented pipeline first preprocesses case documents, extracts legal facts and issue-focused text spans, applies named entity recognition and relation extraction, constructs text-attributed case graphs, generates dense text embeddings, and finally trains a graph neural retrieval model based on edge-aware graph attention. Experimental validation is based on verifiable logs available in the local repository. BM25 achieves Micro-F1 of 0.14, NDCG@5 of 0.32, MRR@5 of 0.30, and MAP of 0.28, while Legal-BERT achieves Micro-F1 of 0.08, NDCG@5 of 0.09, MRR@5 of 0.14, and MAP of 0.05. A legacy CaseGNN-family 2017 graph run reaches stronger graph-based performance, but the best results are obtained by FusionCaseGNN, which achieves best logged year-filtered scores of Micro-F1 0.36, NDCG@5 0.71, MRR@5 0.69, and MAP 0.66. The study concludes that lexical relevance and graph structure are complementary for legal retrieval, and that the proposed fusion-based extension provides the strongest performance among the evaluated methods.

CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background

In common-law systems, previous judicial decisions play a central role in legal reasoning. When a new dispute is heard, lawyers and judges frequently search for earlier cases that discuss similar facts, legal questions, or interpretations. This process is commonly known as precedent retrieval or legal case retrieval. As the number of digitised legal documents grows, manual search becomes increasingly expensive and time consuming. This motivates the use of artificial intelligence and information retrieval techniques for retrieving relevant prior cases automatically.

The present codebase is centred on CaseGNN, a graph-based approach to legal case retrieval. Although the repository README primarily describes COLIEE 2022 and COLIEE 2023 usage, the local workspace clearly includes a 2017 pipeline, 2017 labels, 2017 candidate files, 2017 summary folders, and baseline result logs. For the purpose of this thesis, the study is therefore framed around the FIRE 2017 IRLeD legal precedent retrieval task rather than COLIEE.

The FIRE 2017 IRLeD track defines precedent retrieval as ranking prior cases for a given current case so that actually cited cases appear higher in the ranked list. According to the task description, this is a realistic and challenging legal IR problem because legal documents are long, domain-specific, and structurally complex. The repository under study attempts to address these challenges through a multi-stage pipeline involving text cleaning, issue extraction, relation extraction, dense embedding generation, graph construction, and graph neural retrieval.

1.2 Problem Statement

Purely lexical retrieval methods such as BM25 are effective when relevant cases share overlapping terms, but they often struggle when legal similarity depends on deeper semantic or structural patterns. Dense transformer models can improve semantic matching, but single-vector representations may still underuse the internal relational structure of legal cases. The problem addressed by this thesis is how to model legal cases in a richer way so that semantic content and relational structure are both used during retrieval.

1.3 Research Question

The main research question is:

Can a text-attributed graph neural framework improve legal precedent retrieval on the FIRE 2017 IRLeD task compared with conventional lexical and encoder-based retrieval baselines?

1.4 Objectives

The objectives of this work are:

1. To study and document the architecture of the CaseGNN-based legal retrieval pipeline in the repository.
2. To reinterpret the repository for the FIRE 2017 IRLeD precedent retrieval setting.
3. To describe the preprocessing, information extraction, embedding generation, graph construction, and retrieval model stages in a thesis-ready form.
4. To design and implement a novelty-enhanced extension called FusionCaseGNN based on learnable graph-view fusion and lexical-graph score fusion.
5. To compare BM25, Legal-BERT, CaseGNN, and FusionCaseGNN using the available repository logs.
6. To identify limitations in the current experimental artifacts and suggest future work for a complete evaluation.

1.5 Scope and Delimitations

This thesis draft deliberately excludes LEXA from the empirical scope and does not make any new claims about the full CaseGNN++ research line. The thesis instead focuses on a custom extension built on the current repository code and evaluated on the local FIRE 2017 setup. Specifically, the thesis focuses on:

1. The non-LEXA graph retrieval pipeline represented by `main.py`, `model.py`, `train.py`, and related preprocessing scripts.
2. The FIRE 2017 legal retrieval adaptation available in the local workspace.
3. The thesis contribution named FusionCaseGNN, which adds learnable fact-issue fusion and BM25-graph fusion to the main retrieval path.
4. The verified result logs for BM25, Legal-BERT, the available CaseGNN-family 2017 graph run, and FusionCaseGNN.

1.6 Organisation of the Thesis

Chapter 2 presents the methodology and explains both the original processing pipeline and the proposed FusionCaseGNN extension. Chapter 3 reports the available experimental validation using verified logs and analyses the comparative gains of the new model. Chapter 4 concludes the study and outlines future scope for a final thesis version.

CHAPTER 2. METHODOLOGY

2.1 Overview of the Codebase

The repository implements a modular legal retrieval workflow. The main functional units visible in the codebase are:

1. `PromptCase/preprocessing/` for raw text cleaning and generation of processed fact and issue views.
2. `Information_extraction/` for named entity recognition and relation extraction.
3. `PromptCase/PromptCase_embedding_generation.py` for dense embedding generation.
4. `Graph_generation/TACG.py` for text-attributed case graph construction.
5. `main.py`, `train.py`, and `model.py` for graph neural training and retrieval.
6. `BM25/bm25_retrieval.py` and `LegalBert/Legal_Bert_Retrieval.ipynb` for baseline systems.

The methodology of this thesis is based on reading, extending, and experimentally validating these modules as implemented in the local repository rather than reconstructing the system from the paper alone.

2.2 Dataset Description

This thesis uses the FIRE 2017 IRLeD precedent retrieval framing. The official task description states that for precedent retrieval there are current cases used as queries and prior cases used as retrieval objects, with relevance defined by actual citation links. The evaluation measures mentioned on the task website include Precision, Recall, MAP, DCG, and MRR.

The local workspace appears to use a prepared subset or adapted split of the FIRE 2017 data rather than the full website statistics. The available local artifacts include:

1. `task1_train_2017/task1_train_files_2017/` with 847 files.
2. `task1_test_2017/task1_test_files_2017/` with 168 files.
3. `label/task1_train_labels_2017.json` with 160 labelled training queries.
4. `label/task1_test_labels_2017.json` with 38 labelled test queries.
5. `label/train_2017_candidate_with_yearfilter.json` with 160 candidate lists.
6. `label/test_2017_candidate_with_yearfilter.json` with 40 candidate lists.

Hence, the thesis should interpret the experiments as being conducted on the repository’s prepared 2017 split, which is derived from the FIRE 2017 IRLeD setting but not identical to the full public task statistics reported on the website.

2.3 Preprocessing of Legal Documents

The first methodological stage is text preprocessing. The script `PromptCase/preprocessing/process.py` cleans the raw case files by:

1. Reading files with UTF-8 and Latin-1 fallback.
2. Removing fragment markers and suppression tags.
3. Eliminating citation markers such as bracketed references.
4. Removing translation and formatting artifacts.
5. Performing line-level cleanup and sentence reconstruction.
6. Filtering out French content using language detection.

This stage writes cleaned legal text into the `processed/` directory. The script `PromptCase/preprocessing/reference.py` then creates an issue-focused representation in `processed_new/` by detecting and retaining sentences around suppressed reference markers. It also ensures a minimum text length by augmenting the issue-focused text with context from the processed full text when required.

The design choice here is meaningful for legal retrieval: one text view emphasises factual narrative, while another emphasises issue-bearing and citation-related context.

2.4 Legal Feature Extraction

The repository uses `PromptCase/preprocessing/openaiAPI.py` for abstractive summarisation of long legal text using `distilbart-cnn-12-6`. In the code, long texts are broken into chunks and summarised with a short instruction. The output is stored as summary text and treated as legal facts in later stages.

As a result, each case is effectively represented through at least two complementary textual signals:

1. A fact-oriented summary text.
2. An issue-oriented processed text extracted around citation and reference cues.

This dual representation is important because legal similarity often depends both on factual circumstances and on the legal issues discussed in the judgment.

2.5 Named Entity and Relation Extraction

The next stage transforms text into structured information. In `Information_extraction/knowledge_graph.py`, the pipeline:

1. Loads the transformer-based spaCy English model `en_core_web_trf`.
2. Extracts named entities from each processed legal document.
3. Uses LexNLP act extraction to identify legal acts and statutes.
4. Stores the named entity dictionary in pickle files.
5. Writes the processed text to a `kg/` directory for relation extraction.

Relation extraction is then performed by `Information_extraction/relation_extractor.py`, which calls the Stanford OpenIE pipeline through `process_large_corpus.sh`. This produces relation triplets in CSV form. Together, named entities and extracted relations provide the structured backbone required for graph construction.

2.6 Prompt-Based Text Embedding Generation

The script `PromptCase/PromptCase_embedding_generation.py` generates dense representations using the transformer model `CSHaitao/SAILER_en_finetune`. For each case, it constructs:

1. `fact_text = "Legal facts:" + summary text`
2. `issue_text = "Legal issues:" + issue-focused text`
3. `cross_text = fact_text + issue_text`

The model encodes these three inputs separately and uses the CLS token embedding as the case-level dense vector. These embeddings are saved for later graph construction. This design allows the graph pipeline to inject global semantic information into each graph beyond the local relation structure extracted from text.

2.7 Text-Attributed Case Graph Construction

The graph construction stage is implemented in `Graph_generation/TACG.py`. The script loads:

1. Relation triplets extracted from the fact or issue text.
2. Case-level dense embeddings generated earlier.
3. A case index list aligning embeddings with document identifiers.

The code introduces a virtual node called `promptcase_node`, whose node embedding is the dense PromptCase-style case representation. Relation triplets are then converted into graph nodes and edges, and graph-level data are saved in binary graph files under `Graph_generation/graph/graph_bin_2017/`.

This stage is central to the repository’s methodology. Instead of representing a case as only a sequence or a bag of words, the system models it as a graph where:

1. Nodes capture entities and a global case representation.
2. Edges capture extracted relations.
3. Node and edge features are continuous dense vectors.

Thus, each legal case becomes a text-attributed graph that can be processed by a graph neural network.

2.8 CaseGNN Retrieval Model

The non-extended retrieval model is defined in `model.py` and trained through `main.py` and `train.py`. The architecture uses two `EdgeGATConv` layers from DGL, followed by dropout and graph-level representation extraction.

At training time, the system forms contrastive-style tuples consisting of:

1. A query case.
2. A positive cited case.
3. A random negative case.
4. Optional BM25-based hard negatives.

The model separately encodes fact graphs and issue graphs, normalises the embeddings, and combines similarity scores from both views. Training uses a cross-entropy objective over positive and negative similarity logits with temperature scaling.

At inference time, the system computes pairwise similarity among graph embeddings and ranks the candidate cases for each query. In the original formulation, fact-view similarity and issue-view similarity are added to produce the final score matrix.

In this thesis, the retrieval stage is extended through FusionCaseGNN. Two additional learnable components are introduced in the implemented code:

1. A learnable fact-issue fusion module that replaces fixed equal-weight summation of fact-view and issue-view graph scores.
2. A lexical-graph fusion module that combines the graph similarity score with a normalised BM25 relevance score.

These additions are intentionally lightweight. They preserve the original graph pipeline, require no change to the graph construction stage, and are easy to motivate in legal retrieval. The first addition allows the model to learn whether fact similarity or issue similarity should matter more. The second addition allows the model to retain the strong lexical matching behaviour of BM25 while still benefiting from the structural information captured by the graph encoder.

2.9 Baseline Methods

Two verified baselines are present in the repository:

1. BM25 baseline in `BM25/bm25_retrieval.py`.
2. Legal-BERT retrieval notebook in `LegalBert/Legal_Bert_Retrieval.ipynb`.

The BM25 baseline tokenises lowercased alphanumeric text, computes classic BM25 scores, and ranks candidate documents. It supports ranking across the whole pool and filtering by year-based candidate lists.

The Legal-BERT baseline uses the pretrained model `nlpaueb/legal-bert-base-uncased`. The notebook encodes each case with the CLS representation from the last hidden state and ranks other cases through cosine similarity.

2.10 Evaluation Measures

The repository computes standard legal retrieval metrics through `torch_metrics.py`. The implemented measures include:

1. Micro Precision
2. Micro Recall
3. Micro F1
4. Macro Precision
5. Macro Recall
6. Macro F1
7. NDCG@5
8. MRR@5
9. MAP

The code also supports evaluation with a year-filtered candidate pool through `yf_metric`, which filters predictions using candidate lists such as `test_2017_candidate_with_yearfilter.json`.

3.1 Experimental Setup

This chapter uses only generated logs that can be verified in the local repository and that satisfy the scope restriction of excluding CaseGNN++ and LEXA. The logs used are:

3.2 Available Logs and Inclusion Criteria

For inclusion in this thesis draft, a result log must satisfy both conditions:

1. The method must be within scope, meaning it is not LEXA and not CaseGNN++.
2. The method identity must be directly verifiable from the log content or the generating script.

Under these criteria:

1. BM25 is included.
2. Legal-BERT is included.
3. `CaseGNN2017_run.log` is included as a legacy graph-based 2017 run available in the repository logs, but it should be read with caution because the generating script points to `main_casegnn2plus.py`.
4. `casegnn_novel.log` is included as the present thesis extension built on the current codebase.

In this thesis, the proposed novelty-enhanced model is named `FusionCaseGNN`. The name reflects the two main extensions implemented in the codebase: learnable fusion of fact and issue graph views, and weighted fusion between graph retrieval scores and BM25 scores.

The comparative table shows a clear ranking trend across the available logs. Legal-BERT is the weakest among the four methods. BM25 provides a strong lexical baseline. The earlier graph-based run logged in `CaseGNN2017_run.log` improves substantially over both lexical and encoder-only baselines. However, the best performance is obtained by FusionCaseGNN, which records the highest values on all reported metrics both with and without year filtering. This establishes the proposed novelty as the central empirical contribution of the thesis.

One possible explanation is that the query and relevant prior cases in this split share both lexical overlap and structured relational similarity. BM25 benefits from lexical overlap, while the graph-based systems benefit from entity-relation structure. FusionCaseGNN appears to benefit further from combining these signals instead of relying on either one alone.

3.4 Analysis of Year-Filtered Retrieval

Year filtering improves all methods, but the strongest gain is again observed for FusionCaseGNN. Its best year-filtered NDCG@5 reaches 0.71, MRR@5 reaches 0.69, and MAP reaches 0.66, which are substantially higher than the corresponding values for BM25, Legal-BERT, and the earlier CaseGNN log. This suggests that the proposed fusion strategy is especially effective when the candidate pool is narrowed to more plausible prior cases. It also indicates that lexical evidence and structural evidence are complementary rather than competing signals in the retrieval setting studied here.

3.5 Discussion

The repository is designed around the idea that graph structure should help legal retrieval by representing entities, relations, and global case context jointly. The comparative logs support this intuition. The progression from Legal-BERT to BM25, from BM25 to CaseGNN, and from CaseGNN to FusionCaseGNN suggests that legal retrieval on this split benefits from combining lexical relevance, graph structure, and multi-view scoring.

FusionCaseGNN extends the earlier graph pipeline in two simple but effective ways:

1. It learns how much weight to assign to fact-view and issue-view graph similarities instead of summing them uniformly.
2. It learns how to combine graph-based similarity with BM25 lexical relevance during retrieval.

The learned weights remain close to balanced in the final log, which indicates that both graph views remain useful and that BM25 contributes complementary evidence rather than replacing the graph signal. From a thesis perspective, this is a meaningful finding because it shows that the improvement does not come from collapsing back to a lexical baseline. Instead, the model learns to preserve a mixed retrieval strategy.

3.6 Threats to Validity

Several threats to validity must be acknowledged.

First, the repository README describes COLIEE datasets, while the local workspace contains a 2017 FIRE-style adaptation. This creates a documentation inconsistency that must be clarified in the final thesis.

Second, the local dataset statistics differ from the full FIRE 2017 IRLeD website description. The thesis therefore reports the local workspace configuration rather than the full benchmark distribution.

Third, the currently available `CaseGNN2017_run.log` should be interpreted cautiously because its script identity is not fully aligned with the plain `main.py` CaseGNN path.

Fourth, Legal-BERT is evaluated through a notebook-based baseline, which may not represent the best possible tuned transformer baseline.

Finally, the use of `distilbart-cnn-12-6` for summarisation introduces an external generative preprocessing stage whose prompt design and reproducibility should be documented more fully in a final submission.

CHAPTER 4. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE OF WORK

4.1 Conclusion

This thesis draft documents a legal precedent retrieval pipeline built around graph neural networks and adapted in the present workspace to the FIRE 2017 IRLeD task. The repository implements a rich multi-stage methodology: preprocessing, fact and issue extraction, named entity recognition, relation extraction, dense semantic embedding generation, text-attributed case graph construction, and graph-based retrieval training.

Across the available FIRE 2017 logs, BM25 outperforms Legal-BERT, the graph-based CaseGNN run outperforms both baselines, and FusionCaseGNN achieves the strongest results overall. This shows that strong lexical baselines remain important, but also that structure-aware graph retrieval and lexical-graph fusion can yield substantial improvements when trained successfully.

The main contribution of this thesis draft is the design, implementation, and validation of FusionCaseGNN as a lightweight but effective extension of the existing codebase. In addition to reconstructing the repository into a thesis-ready methodological narrative, the work demonstrates that learnable fusion over fact graphs, issue graphs, and lexical relevance can provide a substantial empirical gain on the local FIRE 2017 split.

4.2 Limitations

The main limitation is that the legacy `CaseGNN2017_run.log` should not be treated as a perfectly clean plain-CaseGNN artifact. A fresh `main.py` log should still be generated in future work for a stricter apples-to-apples comparison.

Another limitation is the mismatch between repository documentation and the actual local dataset usage. A final thesis should resolve this by standardising all file names, scripts, and chapter descriptions around FIRE 2017.

4.3 Future Scope of Work

The following directions are recommended for completing and extending this work:

1. Generate a fresh non-CaseGNN++ run log using `main.py` and include it in the comparison chapter.
2. Add ablation studies comparing fact-only, issue-only, graph-only, BM25-only, and full FusionCaseGNN scoring.
3. Evaluate the effect of BM25 hard negatives in the main FusionCaseGNN training loop.
4. Compare FusionCaseGNN against a stronger tuned transformer retriever.
5. Replace notebook-only baselines with script-based, fully reproducible experiments.
6. Report runtime, GPU requirements, and preprocessing cost in the final thesis.
7. Add error analysis on failed queries to identify whether mistakes arise from facts, issues, lexical mismatch, or relation extraction noise.
8. Explore domain-specific Indian legal language models for better entity and relation representations.
